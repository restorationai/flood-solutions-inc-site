## How much does flooded basement cleanup cost in Sterling Heights, MI?

Most flooded basement cleanups in the Sterling Heights area run from $1,500 for a small, clean-water spot to $15,000 or more for a full sewage backup in a finished space. The spread is wide because "flooded basement" covers very different jobs.

## What actually drives the price up or down?

Five factors decide where your job lands on that table: water category, square footage and depth, finished versus unfinished, drying time, and demo/disposal volume.

**Water category matters more than almost anything else.** Clean water (Category 1), from a supply line or water heater, is the cheapest to remediate because materials can often be dried in place. Gray water (Category 2), like washing machine discharge or groundwater pushing through the foundation under hydrostatic pressure, usually means more porous material comes out. Black water (Category 3), which includes any sewer or drain backup from beyond the trap, sewage, and flood water from outside the home, requires antimicrobial treatment, full PPE for the crew, and far more material removed rather than dried. The [ANSI/IICRC S500 standard](https://iicrc.org/s500/) defines these categories and the industry still uses it to scope a job.

**Square footage and water depth** set the baseline labor and equipment count. A basement with 2 inches of standing water across 800 square feet needs more extraction time, more air movers, and a bigger dehumidifier than a damp corner behind a water heater.

**Finished basements cost more** because carpet, pad, baseboard, drywall (usually cut 12-24 inches above the waterline), and sometimes insulation have to come out and go to disposal. Unfinished basements with a concrete floor and open framing dry faster and cheaper.

**Drying time adds up daily.** Equipment (air movers, LGR dehumidifiers) is typically billed per day, and a job that needs 3-5 days to hit dry-standard costs more than one that clears in 48 hours. ## Will homeowners insurance cover a flooded basement in Sterling Heights?

It depends entirely on what caused the water, not on the fact that your basement flooded. A standard homeowners policy typically covers sudden, accidental water from inside the home, like a burst supply line, but excludes flood water and often excludes sump pump failure and sewer backups unless you added a specific endorsement.

Here's where that split usually falls:

- **Usually covered:** a pipe that suddenly bursts, a water heater that fails, a washing machine hose that lets go.
- **Usually NOT covered without an added endorsement:** a sump pump that overflows or fails, and any sewer or drain backup. Both need a water backup (sewer/sump) endorsement on top of your standard policy.
- **Usually NOT covered by homeowners insurance at all:** water that enters from outside through the foundation, including rising groundwater and surface water from heavy rain, often called seepage or flooding. That requires a separate flood policy through the [National Flood Insurance Program](https://www.fema.gov/flood-insurance) or a private flood carrier.

Coverage language varies by insurer and by policy, so this isn't a guarantee of what your specific plan pays. For the general rules on what a standard policy does and doesn't include, the [Insurance Information Institute's guide to homeowners coverage](https://www.iii.org/article/what-is-covered-by-standard-homeowners-insurance) is a reliable plain-English source, and our own breakdown of [what homeowners insurance covers for water damage](/blog/does-homeowners-insurance-cover-water-damage/) walks through it in more depth. Whatever the cause, document everything with photos before cleanup starts and keep every receipt: insurers expect homeowners to mitigate the loss promptly, and that documentation protects the claim either way.

## What does the cleanup process actually involve, and who should you call?

A flooded basement cleanup moves through extraction, moisture mapping, controlled drying, targeted demo, and documentation, usually in that order over 2 to 5 days depending on the water category and square footage.

1. **Water extraction.** Standing water is pumped or vacuumed out first, often within the first hour or two of arrival.
2. **Moisture mapping.** A moisture meter checks drywall, framing, and flooring to find exactly how far the water traveled, including behind walls where it isn't visible.
3. **Category-appropriate treatment.** Clean water areas get dried in place where possible. Category 2 and 3 losses typically have affected porous materials (carpet, pad, drywall, insulation) removed, plus antimicrobial treatment for anything contaminated.
4. **Structural drying.** Air movers and dehumidifiers run until the space hits a dry standard, verified with follow-up moisture readings, not a guess.
5. **Documentation.** Photos, moisture logs, and a written scope go to you and, if applicable, your insurance adjuster.
