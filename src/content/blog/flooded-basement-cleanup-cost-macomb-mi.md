A Category 3 sewage backup in a finished basement, with drywall, flooring, and contents affected, sits at the high end. The biggest cost drivers are water category, how finished the space is, square footage, and how fast drying starts.

If you're standing at the top of your basement stairs right now looking at an inch of water, you don't need a lecture on drainage systems. You need a number. Macomb County basements flood for a few common reasons: sump pump failure during a power outage, heavy rain overwhelming clay soil, or a sewer line backing up under pressure. Which one happened to you changes the price more than almost anything else.

## How much does flooded basement cleanup cost in Macomb?

Most Macomb homeowners pay between $500 and $15,000 for flooded basement cleanup, with the number landing on either end depending mostly on water category and finish level. A small clean-water event in an unfinished basement can come in under $1,500. A sewage backup in a finished basement with carpet, drywall, and stored belongings can run well past $10,000.

Here's how that typically breaks down by scenario:

| Scenario | Typical cost range |
|---|---|
| Clean water extraction only, unfinished, under 500 sq ft | $500 - $1,500 |
| Full dry-out (air movers, dehumidifiers), unfinished, 500-1,000 sq ft | $1,500 - $4,000 |
| Finished basement, Category 1-2 water, drywall/insulation removed | $3,000 - $8,000 |
| Category 3 sewage backup, unfinished basement | $3,500 - $7,500 |
| Category 3 sewage backup, finished basement with affected contents | $7,000 - $15,000+ |
| Large-loss flood, full flooring/drywall/contents replacement | $10,000 - $25,000+ |

These are typical industry ranges for southeast Michigan, not a quote. Every basement is different, and a written scope after an in-person assessment is the only way to get an exact number for your situation.

## What drives the price up or down?

Four things move the number more than anything else: what category the water is, whether the basement is finished, how many square feet are affected, and how many days it takes to dry.

**Water category** matters because it determines what has to be removed versus what can be dried in place. Clean water (Category 1), like a failed sump pump discharge line, costs less to handle because carpet pad and some drywall can often be saved. Gray water (Category 2), such as washing machine overflow or groundwater seeping through a foundation wall, usually means more materials come out. Black water (Category 3), which includes any sewer backup, flooding from outside the home, or water with feces, always means porous materials get removed and the space gets treated with antimicrobials under the [ANSI/IICRC S500 standard](https://iicrc.org/s500/).

**Finished vs. unfinished** is the next biggest swing. An unfinished basement with a concrete floor and bare framing dries faster and loses less material. A finished basement with drywall, carpet, baseboard, and a drop ceiling has far more to remove, dry, and eventually replace.

**Square footage and drying days** are straightforward: more affected floor space means more equipment and more technician hours. In Macomb's older housing stock near the Clinton River watershed, clay-heavy soil holds water against foundation walls longer after a hard rain, which can stretch drying time if the source isn't fully addressed first.

## Does insurance cover flooded basement cleanup?

It depends entirely on why the basement flooded, not just that it flooded. A standard homeowners policy typically covers sudden, accidental water damage from inside the home, like a burst supply line, but it treats flood water, sewer backup, and sump pump failure very differently.

Flooding from outside the home, meaning surface water, a rising river, or storm water pooling against the foundation, is usually excluded from a standard homeowners policy entirely. That kind of loss needs a separate flood policy, most often through the [National Flood Insurance Program](https://www.fema.gov/flood-insurance). Sewer backup and sump pump overflow are also usually excluded unless you've added a specific water backup endorsement to your policy. Without that endorsement, a sump pump failure during a storm often isn't covered.

This is also why documentation matters before cleanup starts. Photograph the standing water, the waterline on the walls, and any damaged contents before anything gets moved. Our [sump pump insurance post](/blog/does-homeowners-insurance-cover-sump-pump-failure-macomb/) walks through the endorsement question in more detail if that's the scenario you're in.

## What happens during cleanup, step by step?

Flooded basement cleanup generally moves through five phases, in roughly this order:

1. **Source control.** Stop the water, whether that's resetting a sump pump, shutting a valve, or addressing a backed-up line.
2. **Standing water extraction.** Truck-mounted or portable extractors pull out bulk water, usually within the first hour or two on-site.
3. **Category assessment and material removal.** A technician determines the water category and removes anything porous that can't be safely dried (soaked carpet pad, wet drywall below the flood line, saturated insulation).
4. **Structural drying.** Air movers and low-grain-refrigerant dehumidifiers run for several days, with moisture readings checked daily until the framing and remaining materials hit a dry standard.
5. **Cleaning and antimicrobial treatment.** For Category 2 and 3 losses, surfaces get cleaned and treated before anything is closed back up.

Skipping steps, especially drying time, is the most common reason basements end up with mold a few weeks later rather than a few days.

A wet-vac and a box fan handle a small, clean puddle from a one-time appliance leak. Anything beyond that, standing water over an inch deep, any sewage involvement, or water that's been sitting more than 24 hours, calls for professional extraction and drying equipment most homeowners don't own.
