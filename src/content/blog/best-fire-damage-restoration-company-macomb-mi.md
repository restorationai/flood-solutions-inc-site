## What certification should a fire restoration company in Macomb actually hold?

Ask whether the technicians assigned to your job trained specifically on fire and smoke work, not just general water cleanup. The industry benchmark is the [ANSI/IICRC S700 Standard for Professional Fire and Smoke Damage Restoration](https://iicrc.org/s700/), published by the [Institute of Inspection, Cleaning and Restoration Certification](https://www.iicrc.org/), which covers soot behavior, odor sealing, and content cleaning methods that differ by material.

Fire damage isn't just "water damage with ash in it." Soot particles behave differently on plaster than on painted drywall, and the wrong cleaning agent can set an odor permanently instead of removing it. A company that can name the standard it works to is a company that has actually trained for this specific job.

## Does "24/7" actually mean someone answers at 2 a.m.?

A company's emergency claim only matters if a live person picks up the phone the night it happens. Fire damage gets worse by the hour, acidic soot etches metal and glass, and standing water from suppression efforts starts soaking framing immediately.

## Will they board up the property the same day, or send you to someone else?

A fire restoration company worth hiring handles board-up and tarping in-house, on the first visit, without routing you to a separate subcontractor. Open walls and broken windows after a fire invite weather damage, theft, and further structural loss, and every hour without a secure perimeter adds risk.

Ask specifically whether [emergency board-up](/services/emergency-board-up/) is something the crew on site can do immediately, or whether it requires a second appointment. If a company can't answer that clearly, that's a gap worth knowing about before the fire department leaves.

## What happens to furniture, clothing, and other contents that survived?

Salvageable contents need to leave the structure fast, both to get them away from ongoing soot exposure and to get an accurate inventory for your insurance adjuster. A company with a real [content recovery](/services/content-recovery/) process will photograph, box, and log items before they leave your property, then clean and store them separately from the structural work.

Ask how they document contents: is there a written inventory with photos, or do items just disappear into a truck? That documentation is what your adjuster uses to settle the contents portion of your claim, and it's also your protection if something gets damaged, lost, or disputed later.

## How should the estimate separate cleanup costs from rebuild costs?

A trustworthy estimate lists mitigation work (demolition of damaged materials, odor treatment, contents handling, equipment) as one line item and rebuild work (drywall, flooring, paint, cabinetry) as a separate one, because insurers often process and pay these two categories differently. If a company hands you one lump number with no breakdown, you have no way to verify it against your policy's actual coverage.

Be cautious of any company that pushes you to sign an assignment of benefits (AOB) before you've seen a scope of work. An AOB hands your insurance claim rights to the contractor, and once it's signed, you often lose the ability to negotiate directly with your own insurer. The [Insurance Information Institute's guide to settling claims after a disaster](https://www.iii.org/article/settling-insurance-claims-after-a-disaster) is a useful reference before you sign anything, and Michigan's [Department of Insurance and Financial Services](https://www.michigan.gov/difs) is the state agency to contact if a claim dispute comes up. For the immediate hours right after a fire, the [American Red Cross guide on what to do after a home fire](https://www.redcross.org/get-help/how-to-prepare-for-emergencies/types-of-emergencies/fire/home-fire-recovery.html) covers safety steps before any restoration crew arrives.
