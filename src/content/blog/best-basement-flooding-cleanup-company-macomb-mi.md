## What should you look for in a basement flooding cleanup company?

The right company combines fast response, the correct certification for water damage work, and a process for documenting the loss so your insurer doesn't dispute it later. Those three things matter more than a flashy website or a low quoted price.

A basement flood is almost never "just water." Depending on the source, it can be clean water from a supply line, gray water from a sump pump backup, or Category 3 black water from a sewer line, each requiring a different response under the [ANSI/IICRC S500 standard](https://iicrc.org/s500/). A company that treats every basement flood the same way, regardless of source, is skipping a step that protects your health and your claim.

Ask directly: do your technicians hold water damage restoration certification, and will you tell me in writing what category of water you found? A company that answers plainly, without hedging, is one worth hiring.

## Is 24/7 response actually available, or is it a slogan?

Standing water in a basement starts causing secondary damage, swelling baseboards, wicking into drywall, within hours, so the company's real response hours matter more than the word "emergency" on their homepage.

Many restoration companies list 24/7 service but route after-hours calls to voicemail. Before you hire anyone, call the number at 9 p.m. or on a Sunday and see what actually happens. ## Will the company work directly with your insurance company?

A company that documents the loss properly, with moisture readings, photos, and a written scope of work, gives your insurance adjuster what they need to process the claim without back-and-forth delays.

Basement flooding sits in a gray area for a lot of homeowners' policies. A sudden pipe failure is typically covered. A sump pump failure or sewer backup usually is NOT covered unless you added a water backup endorsement to your policy, and rising groundwater or surface flooding generally needs a separate flood policy through the [National Flood Insurance Program](https://www.fema.gov/flood-insurance). A company that tells you this upfront, rather than promising blanket coverage, is being honest with you. If you're not sure what your policy covers, our breakdown on [sump pump failure coverage in Macomb County](/blog/does-homeowners-insurance-cover-sump-pump-failure-macomb/) explains the endorsement question in plain terms.

Ask the company: will you provide a detailed moisture log and photo documentation I can hand to my adjuster? If they hesitate, that's a red flag.

## What questions should you ask before anyone arrives?

Ask five things on the first call: are your technicians what category of water are you expecting based on what I'm describing, do you provide a written scope before starting work, how do you bill (time and materials versus a flat estimate), and will you coordinate directly with my insurance adjuster.

A company with nothing to hide answers all five without pushing you toward a same-day signature. Get the scope in writing before extraction equipment comes off the truck. If a company won't put anything on paper until after the work is done, keep calling.

It's also worth asking what equipment they'll actually use. Basement flooding, especially Category 2 or 3 water, often requires removing porous materials like carpet pad and drywall below the waterline rather than attempting to dry them in place. A company that proposes drying everything without removing anything contaminated may be cutting corners.

## What happens after you call

Once you've picked a company, a technician should arrive, assess the water category and the extent of the damage, and start extraction immediately, since every hour standing water sits increases the risk of mold, which the [EPA notes can begin growing within 24 to 48 hours](https://www.epa.gov/mold/brief-guide-mold-moisture-and-your-home) on wet materials. From there, expect containment of any affected porous materials, placement of air movers and dehumidifiers, and a drying plan with daily moisture checks until the space reads dry on a meter, not just dry to the touch.
