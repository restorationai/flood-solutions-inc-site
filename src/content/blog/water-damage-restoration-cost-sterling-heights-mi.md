**TL;DR:** Water damage restoration in Sterling Heights, MI typically costs $3 to $12 or more per square foot, with small single-room jobs running $500 to $3,000 and full basement losses often landing between $3,000 and $15,000. The biggest cost drivers are the water's contamination level (Category 1, 2, or 3), how much drywall and flooring has to come out, and how fast drying starts.

If a sump pump failed overnight or a supply line let go behind your dishwasher, the number you want right now is simple: what is this actually going to cost? Sterling Heights homes, especially the ranches and split-levels built through the 1970s and 80s near the Clinton River watershed, tend to have finished or semi-finished basements that take the brunt of these losses. The price swings more than people expect, and the reason almost always comes down to how contaminated the water was and how long it sat before drying started.

## How Much Does Water Damage Restoration Cost in Sterling Heights, MI?

Most Sterling Heights homeowners pay between $3 and $12 per square foot for professional water damage restoration, with the total depending heavily on whether the water was clean, gray, or black. A single affected room with clean water might run $500 to $3,000; a full basement loss with contaminated water and demolition can climb past $10,000.

Here's how that breaks down by scope:

| Scope of the Job | Typical Cost Range |
|---|---|
| Water extraction only, single room, Category 1 (clean) water | $150 - $500 |
| Drying equipment (air movers + dehumidifiers), per day | $200 - $600/day, usually 3-5 days |
| Drywall removal and replacement after a leak (per 100 sq ft) | $1,000 - $3,000 |
| Basement restoration, Category 1 clean water | $3 - $5 per sq ft |
| Category 2 (gray water) cleanup | $4 - $7 per sq ft |
| Category 3 (black water / sewage) cleanup | $7 - $12+ per sq ft |

These are typical industry ranges for the region, not a quote. Every loss is different, and a written scope should be the basis for any number you're given.

## What Factors Drive the Price Up or Down?

The water's contamination category matters more than almost anything else on the invoice. [Category 1, 2, and 3 water](https://iicrc.org/s500/) each require different handling under the IICRC S500 standard, and the category decides whether materials get dried in place or torn out entirely.

Category 1 (clean water) comes from a sanitary source like a supply line or a water heater, and it can often be dried without removing drywall or flooring, as long as drying starts within 24 to 48 hours. Category 2 (gray water), such as washing machine discharge or groundwater seeping through a foundation wall, usually means removing baseboards and some lower drywall. Category 3 (black water), which includes any sewer or drain backup from beyond the trap and rising flood water, almost always means tearing out porous materials like carpet, pad, and affected drywall, plus antimicrobial treatment. For more on what qualifies, see our [Category 3 water damage breakdown for Sterling Heights](/blog/category-3-water-damage-sterling-heights-mi/).

Other factors that move the number: square footage affected, how many days drying equipment runs, how much drywall and flooring needs replacing, and whether mold has already started (mold can begin growing within 24 to 48 hours on wet drywall per the [EPA](https://www.epa.gov/mold/brief-guide-mold-moisture-and-your-home)). Response time is the one factor homeowners actually control. A basement that sits wet for three days before anyone calls almost always costs more than one addressed the same day.

## Will Homeowners Insurance Cover the Cost?

Most standard homeowners policies cover sudden, accidental water damage, like a burst pipe or a supply line that fails without warning, but they do not automatically cover sewer backups or sump pump failure. Those need a separate water backup (sewer/sump) endorsement added to the policy.

Gradual leaks, long-term seepage, and flood water from outside the home (rising rivers, surface water, storm surge) are typically excluded from a standard policy entirely. Flood damage from an outside water source generally requires a separate flood policy through the [National Flood Insurance Program](https://www.floodsmart.gov/) or a private flood carrier. Check your policy's declarations page for a water backup endorsement before you assume sump pump failure is covered; this is one of the most common claim denials we see. Our full breakdown lives at [Does Homeowners Insurance Cover Water Damage?](/blog/does-homeowners-insurance-cover-water-damage/) and we covered the Sterling Heights-specific cost angle in more depth in [Flooded Basement Cleanup Cost in Sterling Heights, MI](/blog/flooded-basement-cleanup-cost-sterling-heights/).

When a loss is covered, documentation is what gets it paid. Photos before anything is touched, moisture readings logged daily, and a written scope broken out by category and square footage all protect the claim and give the adjuster what they need to approve it.

## What Does the Restoration Process Actually Involve?

The cost mostly reflects three phases: extraction, drying, and repair, each billed differently because each requires different labor and equipment.

1. **Inspection and water categorization.** A technician identifies the water category and class, checks for moisture behind walls with a meter, and documents the extent of the damage with photos.
2. **Extraction.** Standing water is removed with pumps or truck-mounted extractors. This is usually the smallest line item.
3. **Drying.** Air movers and low-grain-refrigerant (LGR) dehumidifiers run for 3 to 5 days typically, following [IICRC S500](https://iicrc.org/s500/) drying standards. This is billed per day and is often the biggest cost driver on a clean-water loss.
4. **Demolition and disposal (when needed).** Wet drywall, insulation, carpet pad, and baseboards that can't be dried are removed and disposed of. This is almost always required on Category 2 and 3 losses.
5. **Repair.** Drywall, baseboards, and flooring are replaced to match the pre-loss condition.
